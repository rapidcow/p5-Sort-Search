       (continued) Introduction to Sort::Search
            ((now for actually "search"ing))

                       * * *

So the more traditional statement of binary search is a
particular case of "linear search" -- or search plainly.
The common way the problem is framed is with finding a
needle in a haystack, which helps with the imagery, so
I'll go with that.

(Concretely though, it's just elements in an array. ;P)

So how does binary search help with searching for a needle
in a haystack?  The key insight is that we can convert the
needle into a PREDICATE we can apply one of the bisection
methods from the preceding section to, provided that elements
of the haystack form a TOTAL ORDER.  That's the math term for
data types that are totally comparable by some comparison
function, thus endowed with the notion of "small to large".
For example, integers can be ordered by the comparison function:

   { ( $a > $b )     When negative (-), $a is LESS THAN  $b
    - ( $a < $b ) }  When     zero (0), $a is EQUAL  to  $b
  OR { $a <=> $b }   When positive (+), $a is GREATER THAN $b

One may check that this comparison function satisfies the
axioms of a (strict) total order:

   (Antisymmetry)  It is never the case that `$a < $b' and
                   `$b < $a' are simultaneously true.
                                  [True for DISTINCT $a,$b.]

   (Connectivity)  Either `$a < $b' or `$b > $a' holds.
      [totality]                  [True for DISTINCT $a,$b.]

   (Transitivity)  Chaining inequalities makes sense.
                   (So `$a < $b && $b < $c' means `$a < $c')
                                    [True for ANY $a,$b,$c.]

Antisymmetry and connectivity together are equivalent
to the following condition (which arguably is THE most
important manifestation of a total order):

   (Trichotomy)   Exactly ONE of the following holds:
                  `$a < $b',   `$a == $b',  `$a > $b'.
                                       [True for ANY $a,$b.]

An array is SORTED when elements are arranged "small to large"
or "large to small" (the former being sorted in "increasing
order" and the latter in "decreasing order"). A sorted integer
array of the first 13 decimal digits of \pi is therefore:

    3. 1  4  1  5  9  2  6  5  3  5  8  9
   [1  1  2  3  3  4  5  5  5  6  8  9  9]   (increasing)
   [9  9  8  6  5  5  5  4  3  3  2  1  1]   (decreasing)

Now consider the increasing order, and a "needle" we want to
find in it -- "5", I'll say.  Let's compare each element in
this haystack against the needle.  (Note that trichotomy
assures me that the comparison is always well-defined.)

   [1  1  2  3  3  4  5  5  5  6  8  9  9]       x
    -  -  -  -  -  -  0  0  0  +  +  +  +    cmp(x, 5) := (x <=> 5)

It appears that the negatives, zeros, and positives are well-
behaved, each taking a sturdy contiguous chunk of the array.
Is this a coincidence?  Let's see: let `k' be the needle....

 * Do negatives ALWAYS precede zeros?  If `i' were a negative
   and `j' were a zero, then `j == k' and `i < k'.  By law of
   substitution, then, `i < j'.  So we know any zero `i' must
   follow all negatives `j'.

 * Do zeros ALWAYS precede positives?  If `i' were a zero and
   and `j' were a positive, then `j > k' and `i == k'.  By law
   of substitution again, `j > i'.  So any positive `j' *indeed*
   follows any zero `i' -- i.e., any zero precedes any positive.

Notice that so far, the behavior of the comparison benefits
from the transitivity of equality as an equivalence relation
(in the guise of substitution, but I'll spare you the full
details of logic... ;)

Now we need to just check ONE more case...

 * Do negatives ALWAYS precede positives?  If `i' were a
   negative and `j' were a positive, then `i < k' and `j > k',
   or `i < k' and `k < j' -- it's transitivity!  Transitivity
   gives us `i < j' for free.  (Or if you prefer chaining it,
   you can write `i < k < j' and strike out the `< k <' part.)

So in fact the negatives, zeros, and positives DO look like
that for ANY needle we pick.  (The blocks may be empty but
that's something I will discuss in a bit.)

We call such a well-defined mapping into contiguous blocks
an ORDERING on the array (the -ING is important, because
terminology has stuck, eh....)  What we have shown is that
the comparison function that characterizes the total order
itself is a VALID ordering, but orderings in general need
not be the comparison function; namely, we may consider a
larger set of values "equal", such as a *range* of needles.
(This is what most languages like to conflate when binary
search is WAY cooler than that!)

Either way, what DOES an ordering give us?

#                         |
